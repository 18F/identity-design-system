The library keeps a map of the neighborhood by its front door. Each Friday, someone adds a pin for a place worth visiting.

This week, a blue pin marks the garden behind the old train station. The note beside it says to look for the pear tree near the gate.

On Saturday morning, Mara takes the note off the map to copy the directions. The librarian lends her a pencil and turns the card over. Someone has drawn the gate on the back, right down to the crooked latch.

The station is easy to find. The gate is harder. Mara walks past it twice before she notices a pear on the pavement. A second pear sits on the low brick wall. Just beyond it, almost hidden by the leaves, is the latch from the drawing.

Inside, a man is repairing a bench. He asks her to sit on one end while he tightens a bolt at the other. They test it together. No wobble. From the bench she can see the station clock, stopped at twenty minutes past four.

When Mara mentions the map, the man wipes his hands and goes to the tool shed. He comes back with a photograph of the same view. The tree in the picture is barely taller than the bench. On the back, in faded ink, someone has written the date of the last passenger train.

She asks if the library can borrow the photograph for a week. He finds an envelope, prints his name on it, and adds one instruction: “Please bring it back on a dry day.” She slips it between the pages of her notebook for the walk home.

By Monday the photograph is on the map beside the blue pin. Mara adds a line to the directions, too: “If you reach the clock, you have gone a little too far. Turn around. The gate is much easier to see on the way back.”

On Tuesday a woman stops at the photograph on her way to return a book. She recognizes the white shape beside the station door. It is not a sack of letters, as Mara guessed, but a dog named Pip. She remembers because he used to ride home in the basket on her father's bicycle.

The librarian puts a little stack of blank cards beneath the map. By Thursday there are three different accounts of who planted the pear tree. Nobody agrees on the year, but all three cards mention the yellow bucket the gardener used to carry water from the station tap.

Friday is dry. Mara takes the photograph down and walks it back to the garden, with the new cards tucked into the envelope. The man reads the one about Pip twice. Then he takes a pencil from behind his ear and draws a small bicycle beside the dog.

Before she leaves, he gives her two pears from the tree. One is for the librarian. The other barely makes it past the gate. Back at the library, Mara leaves the blue pin where it is and puts the crooked-latch drawing beside it for the next person to borrow.
